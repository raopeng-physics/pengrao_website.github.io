I have been reading Sam Freedman's 'Failed State, Why Britain Doesn't Work and How We Fix it'. It is the most insightful book I have read in a long time. The writer, as a former Tory policy advisor, argues against the dismantling of the British state, the very process he contributed to, and his arguments combined clarity, precision and a focus on structural reasons instead of, e.g. the personal flaws of politicians.

Interestingly, several key observations in his book, the retreat of the state in society, the evasion of Parliamentary and democratic scrutiny by the executive branch, and the influence of news media, are clearly general trends within advanced liberal democratic states, albeit the peculiarities of Britain exacerbated these developments. Indeed, already in 1994 Hobsbawm wrote on the impasse of liberal democratic politics:

>Increasingly, however, governments took to by-passing both the electorate and its representative assemblies... relying on the volatility, divisions or inertness of public opinions. Politics increasingly became an exercise in evasion, as politicians were afraid of telling voters what they did not want to hear... Even in democratic countries more and more decision making bodies would be withdrawn from electoral control... Centralising governments, such as those in Britain in the 1980s and early 1990s, were particularly inclined to multiply such ad hoc authorities not answering to an electorate and nicknamed "quangos" [originated from the word 'quasi-NGOs']. (E. Hobsbawn, 1994, p580)

These developments come from the relative and total loss of state capacities since the 1980s, in conducting independent policies against a more globalised world economy which it can no longer control, in maintaining the welfare programs that were expanded decades earlier, and in the retreat of state and public institutions from society that accompanied the privatisation of entertainment and life-style. This has in turn fractured democratic politics and made it more volatile, as the majority of the electorate still expected the state to fulfill these functions, and as it was no longer represented by traditional mass parties which were the mechanism to reflect its wishes. These are universal trends in advanced capitalist countries of the West; in Britain and the USA they are deemed ideologically desirable.

However, the roll-back of the state in its traditional roles leads to its strengthening in several other areas, in the growing number of policy-making bodies, usually unelected, that became indispensable in maintaining the daily functioning of the states in a much more unfavourable environment, especially pertaining to provision of social services, regulatory bodies and surveillance. The associated unpredictability and explosiveness of the electorate, exacerbated by the media, means that these functions must be shielded away from them and sometimes democratic politics altogether, in addition to the major decisions that will always be unpopular, i.e. relating to issues of European Integration. Of course another factor is that, increasingly, the questions that face the executive governments are so complex that sometimes they are simply beyond the expertise of the elected representatives. Therefore, these functions are steadily taken away from the grasp of local governments, which explains the centralising instincts of British governments that Freedman observed in his book. At the same time, as Freedman noted succinctly, the drive to centralisation is profoundly self-defeating, as an already weakened state cannot cope with the additional functions and roles it accumulates for itself, and the resulting inefficiencies and dysfunction merely reinforce the belief in the need for centralising further.

Given that the state cannot rule in the usual sense as was understood in the past, and that the electorate still expects it to, it must increasingly engage in symbolic legislature and politically charged acts which `demonstrate' that they are still ruling. The encumbered great mass parties of the past, their membership and vote shares dwindling as the public withdraws from politics en masse while their supporter base becomes more politicised, are particularly inclined to engage in such political theater. Even more so are the smaller parties of sectional interests, whose method of mobilization lies in appealing to personal lifestyles, mostly of middle-class urban youth. However, improvised policies often cannot accomplish their stated goals, if anything they tend to be less effective, so their cynicism is plain even to those who support them. In addition, because these policies are often ad hoc inventions to deal with the enormous public pressure, usually from minority interest groups with nationalistic or social-identitarian ideologies, to which all democratic governments feel compelled to respond, they are not genuinely popular. Therefore, in the end, the effect of such political posturing has paradoxically been to accelerate the alienation of the public from traditional political processes even further. 

Amidst the confusion, cultural anxieties and demoralisation in the middle of an economic downturn, the social conditions are ripe for mobilising of the depoliticised outside the modus operandi of traditional electoral politics, i.e. the rise of the far-right parties which have far less scruple to exploit the desperate emotions and hysteria precisely because they are not concerned with upkeeping the functioning of the state noted earlier. It is no accident that both far-right movements that had seized power in major states, i.e. post-Brexit Britain and the Trump administration in the USA, predominantly came into conflicts with the depoliticised governmental apparatus and civil service.

As for the remedies offered in Freedman's book, for example the restoration of local power centers, it is unclear if the objective conditions for them will be present; the current economic downturn in Europe and the US is expected to continue as their industries are squeezed by China which has made exporting overcapacity a manifest goal to offset its own domestic slump. But even if economic prosperity is to return, and such a prospect remains very unlikely, the profound social transformation makes a return to the old political models impractical. However, at least one thing is clear, namely that from here any step forward must involve the regulation and structuring of social expressions (in a way this is achieved by the far right by their organised use of media infrastructure, which has turned them into a politically formidable force), and a restoration of public authority in some form. The most obvious candidates are the supranational organisations such as the European Union. However, the impasse of EU integration has demonstrated the difficulty in continuing in this direction, and the progress made so far is owed explicitly to side-stepping the democratic process, to making EU policy-making, especially those of the European Central Bank, beyond democratic control. This cannot be a suitable basis for going forward.
